Introduction

As I was building out team pages and conducting research on whether it was possible to quantify team tactics (teams don't fall into separate types of tactics; styles DO sit on a sliding scale of possession and direct play though - more on this in another post), e.g. tiki-taka, low block, gegenpressing and other popular tactical styles you have heard - using event data, I found out that team pressing doesn’t really measure the way a team plays or a certain tactical style, it mostly reflects how strong a team is. The reverse is also true with a team that sits back; they are often weaker. After finding this out my guiding question then was: Is pressing a tactical decision by coaches knowing their teams are weak/strong? Or is sitting low an unsuccessful tactic in aggregate and pressing a trait that leads to winning? Answering that turned out to need two pieces. This one covers the first half: what pressing numbers measure in MLS, and why MLS looks so different from leagues abroad. Whether pressing wins, and whether it follows the coach or the club, is Part 2. A few surprising results from follow-up questions came up from trying to answer this initial question. This was planned to be an MLS-only piece, but the questions became easier to answer with larger sample sizes. This piece will cover some aspects of foreign leagues while twisting that into an MLS focus.

Important note on the data: Most of this research looks at minutes when the score is level (more on that in the notes).

Section 1: In MLS, where a team wins the ball depends on how good it is and how good its opponent is. How hard it presses depends mostly on itself.

In MLS 2020-2026, it was found that pressing is less of a tactical style and more a reflection of how good a team itself is AND how strong the opponent that team faces is, though the opponent matters much more for where a team wins the ball than for how hard it presses. On the left you can see two measures of a team’s “pressing.” Line height (how far from its own goal a team wins the ball back, on average) and PPDA (passes per defensive action, how many passes an opponent makes in their own 60% of the field divided by the pressing team’s tackles, challenges, interceptions, or fouls in that same 60%). Line height is higher when a team's average xG difference per match over the season is higher and is lower when a team’s xG difference is lower. In simpler words, stronger teams win the ball closer to the opponent’s goal and weaker teams win the ball closer to their own end of the pitch. PPDA follows the same pattern. A stronger team presses harder: the opponent gets fewer passes for each tackle, challenge, interception or foul. Weaker teams allow more passes for each defensive action.

On the right side (red lines) we have the same two measures with every team compared with the strength of their opponent, so it shows how each of these two measures changes for each team from one opponent to the next. What was found was that their pressing behavior changed by opponent strength. So when strong teams play strong teams, they win the ball closer to their own goal and press less intensely. But this is true for a team of any strength playing stronger or weaker teams. No matter the strength of themselves, they win the ball back further towards their own goal and have a higher PPDA against stronger teams. Those metrics trend the opposite way against weaker teams.

So what's the key finding? In MLS, better teams win the ball higher up the pitch: about 4.4 meters higher for every goal of xG difference per match. On the right, measuring one team across a season: for every goal better its opponent is, it wins the ball about 3 meters closer to its own goal. 3 is about two-thirds of 4.4, and that's the 69%. In other words, the opponent's strength moves a team's line height about two-thirds as much as the team's own strength does. A big part of what looks like "this team wins the ball high" is really "this team is playing someone worse this week." Pressing intensity (PPDA) is different: only 38% of the gap shows up when the opponent changes, so how hard a team presses sticks with the team more and depends on the opponent less. That's where the research got interesting, because in most of the other leagues I looked at, the PPDA gap is close to or above 100%. That segues us into the next section.

Section 2: In other leagues, a team's pressing changes with the opponent about as much as pressing differs between strong and weak teams. In MLS, PPDA changes much less.

The data in the chart above now shows what we just learned specific to MLS (the 69% for line height and 38% for PPDA) compared to the other 11 leagues I have data from. Line height for MLS is mostly on par with what we see for other leagues. A team's own strength matters a bit more than the opponent's, but the opponent still counts for a lot of explaining line height. MLS fits in with the other leagues here. Where things really get interesting is comparing PPDA to the other 11 leagues. In almost every other league, a team's PPDA moves with the opponent's strength as much as or more than it differs between strong and weak teams. In other words, outside MLS, the opponent's strength matters as much as the team's own, or more (93–161%, except the Eredivisie). A team's PPDA drops against weak opponents and rises against strong ones, so PPDA follows the matchup. In MLS, it mostly follows how good you are. The opponent's effect is only about 38% as big, so an MLS team's pressing intensity changes much less from one opponent to the next. Only the Eredivisie comes somewhat close to MLS’s tendency of the opponent changing a team's PPDA much less. MLS is clearly the outlier on PPDA. So the natural next question to ask is: Why doesn't pressing in MLS follow the opponent the way it does everywhere else?

Section 3: Abroad, a weak team facing a better opponent stops passing around at the back. In MLS, weak teams keep doing it.

The chart above is showing the shift in a team’s behavior towards passing less or more in their own 60% of the pitch when adjusting for opponent strength. In MLS, strong and middle teams change their passing at the back by about 9% and 7% between their weakest and strongest opponents, about what strong teams do abroad (8%). Middle teams in MLS don’t quite follow the trend abroad and adjust less. The interesting wrinkle is that when we look at weak teams in MLS, they barely adjust their playing out from the back taking opponent strength into consideration; only about 3%, where weak teams abroad change by 17%: they pass it around at the back less. As a reminder PPDA can be expressed as:

PPDA =Opponent's passes in its own 60%Tackles + Interceptions + Challenges + Fouls committed

So the smaller PPDA swing in section 2 comes mostly from the top of that fraction. Weak MLS teams keep making passes in their own end against stronger opponents, so the team pressing them sees about as many passes as usual, and its PPDA barely moves.

A reader familiar with MLS may argue that because of parity, MLS's weak teams aren't as far behind its best teams as weak teams abroad are. Even counting only opponents within MLS's usual range of strength, weak teams abroad still pass about 17% less at the back against their strongest opponents. So do weak teams in the 2. Bundesliga and Brasileirão, the two leagues closest to MLS in how evenly matched their teams are. Weak MLS teams have more of the ball than weak teams abroad (48% possession vs 45%), their passes go forward less, and fewer of their passes are long balls (11.8% vs 14.2%). MLS's weak teams change the least of any of the 12 leagues, with only Portugal close. So once again we are left with another why question. Unfortunately this is not as simple as our previous one.

Section 4: Why do weak MLS teams keep playing out, and what to do about it?

Spoiler: this section will be a little more like an appendix in nature since there is no clear answer as to why weak MLS teams keep passing out from the back against stronger opponents.

4a. This pattern in MLS teams isn’t new

I took a look at 2013-2019 data as well, and MLS’s weak teams adapted as little in that time period (+7% shift in passing at the back). As a whole the league has been trending towards playing shorter passes as well: the portion of long balls making up all passes in open play went from 12.4% in 2020 to 9.4% in 2026. I suspect we will see the same phenomenon in 2027.

4b. What doesn’t cause or explain weaker teams passing out from the back

We already covered parity as a non-explanation in section 3. I had a hypothesis that perhaps this is a cultural style of play that North Americans emphasize over European or South American coaches. I took all coaches from the bottom-third teams in MLS from 2013 to 2026 and looked at where they coached before their first MLS head-coach job. I split these coaches into a North American and abroad group. The results show that this is also a nonfactor in explaining this tendency. North American and abroad coaches had nearly the same change (+5% adjustment for North American coaches and +3% adjustment for abroad coaches) in passing at the back when adjusting for the strength of the opponent. I also wondered whether players coming from abroad would have a playstyle culture leak through and that doesn’t seem to be the case. Teams in the middle and top thirds for build-up passes by players signed from abroad show the same minimal adjustment we see in MLS in general. Ironically, teams that relied least on players signed from abroad adapted a little more (+8%), though not enough to call it a real difference.

4c. Does playing out from the back hurt or help weaker teams?

Whenever I conduct research, I think it’s important to answer a potential reader’s question of “why should I care?” So, with that in mind, I was curious whether the weaker side in a match playing out led to better outcomes. Abroad (2017–2026), the weaker side in a match that plays out picks up a little more in points than the matchup predicts, about one point a season. They don't create more chances. Over the same seasons in MLS, playing out makes no real difference to the chances underdogs create or give up. Points may tick up slightly, under half a point a season, but the data can't confirm it. One wrinkle: MLS underdogs that play out the most do give up more chances from losing the ball in their own end, without making more from their own possessions at the back. Over a whole match it evens out, so the cost seems to be made up elsewhere.

4d. Additional quick hit thoughts and explanations

  • In MLS there is no relegation, lowering the stakes for weaker teams and lowering the value of points later in the season for teams already out of the playoff race. These teams abroad may be more desperate to pick up an extra point by playing it safe, launching long balls up the field to relieve pressure. This is something I plan to test: compare teams abroad near the drop with safe mid-table teams of the same strength. More follow-up items to come below.
  • Coaching education and academies: Does the education for players and/or coaches in MLS academies and U.S./Canadian youth systems emphasize playing out from the back?
  • A possibility on the pressing side: strong MLS teams step up their press against weaker opponents a bit less than teams abroad, which could make playing out feel less risky. But when the press does win the ball, MLS teams get as much out of it as teams anywhere.

4e. Potential action items

Defender Recruiting Abroad

I initially hypothesized that MLS teams may be more interested in recruiting secure defenders (those who turn over the ball in their own end less) from abroad since this seems to be a cultural way of play in MLS. Hypothetically, these players would stylistically fit better in MLS. I tested whether ball security is a quantifiable and reliable skill. Hint: it is. Defenders who lose the ball less in their own third than expected keep doing it after a club change (r 0.46; center backs alone 0.31). I also tested whether a more secure back line (one that turns the ball over less) is worth much on the field. Hint: it isn’t. A more secure back line adds under a hundredth of an xG a match, and MLS is no different from abroad. What this doesn't answer is whether defenders who move the ball forward (rather than just keep it) are worth more in MLS. I’d measure that in a different way and that is a potential follow-up study to this.

Match preparation for coaches, performance analysts, and anyone working with pressing metrics

This is somewhat obvious but no matter who your opponent is in MLS, coaches and staff should be ready for that team to play out from the back. They're unlikely to switch to long balls, even when outmatched. Also, when assessing pressing numbers in MLS keep the opponent in mind. Most of what a team's pressing numbers tell you is how good it is. In MLS, where a team wins the ball also depends heavily on who it's playing. PPDA depends on the opponent less here than anywhere else I looked, so in MLS it says more about the team itself than its opponent.

4f. Potential follow-up studies

  • Relegation pressure abroad and whether this has an impact on playing out from the back or launching long balls.
  • Check whether ball-playing defenders are worth more in MLS than abroad.
  • Pressure itself. Event data logs no pressure events, so "under pressure" is approximated. Tracking data would let us measure press resistance directly.
  • Bigger picture: does pressing win? And does it follow the coach or the club? (Part 2 of this blog post, to be posted soon).

Notes and Caveats

About the data: Opta event data via WhoScored. MLS regular seasons are available from 2013; the 11 foreign leagues from 2016–17. When I compare MLS with leagues abroad, I use matching seasons: 2020–2026 in sections 1–3 and 2017–2026 in section 4c. Section 4a puts MLS 2013–19 next to abroad 2016–19, the earliest foreign seasons I have. Questions about MLS alone (section 4b) use every season available.

Score state: unless I say otherwise, the numbers in this piece only count minutes when the score is level. Teams play differently when they're ahead or behind (a team chasing a game pushes up and goes long more), so leaving those minutes out keeps score effects from looking like style. Two exceptions: the long-ball trend in 4a counts all open play, and in 4c the playing-out style is measured at level scores but the points and chances it's judged on cover the whole match.

PPDA was introduced by Colin Trainor: "Defensive Metrics: Measuring the Intensity of a High Press," StatsBomb, July 30, 2014. https://www.hudl.com/blog/defensive-metrics-measuring-the-intensity-of-a-high-press
